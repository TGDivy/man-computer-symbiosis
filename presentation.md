N–COMPUTER SYMBIOSIS

## A lost film about the future of thinking

You are not merely making a slide presentation.

You are acting as:

* presentation director
* visual designer
* researcher
* archival image researcher
* motion/transition designer
* slightly mischievous documentary filmmaker

Your task is to create a highly bespoke presentation inspired primarily by J. C. R. Licklider's 1960 paper **“Man–Computer Symbiosis.”**

Primary source:

https://groups.csail.mit.edu/medg/people/psz/Licklider.html

Read the entire paper before designing the presentation.

Do not rely on summaries of it.

---

# 1. THE IDEA

The presentation should feel like we have discovered an obscure **1960s instructional/science film about the future**, restored it, and are screening it for a modern audience.

It should not feel like:

* a corporate PowerPoint
* an AI conference presentation
* a history lecture
* a Wikipedia summary
* a chronology of computer inventions
* “Look at all these things a guy predicted!”
* a generic retro template with some film grain added

It should feel like an **art-directed short film that happens to be presented through slides**.

The visual and tonal universe should draw from things like:

* 1950s/60s scientific films
* Bell Labs educational films
* old NASA engineering material
* Cold War information films
* typewritten research reports
* oscilloscope displays
* punch cards
* graph paper
* reel-to-reel tape
* microfilm
* library index cards
* early computing laboratories
* technical diagrams
* projector leaders and reel-change marks
* *Dr. Strangelove*-style deadpan institutional seriousness
* *2001: A Space Odyssey*-era machine aesthetics
* *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* in attitude: cosmic ideas presented with a certain cheerful absurdity

Do not imitate any one film directly.

Borrow the **visual grammar and attitude**, not copyrighted frames.

---

# 2. THE CENTRAL ARGUMENT

The presentation is not fundamentally about Licklider predicting modern technology.

Its central idea is:

> **Computers are most interesting when they reduce the distance between interesting thoughts.**

The machine does not have to replace thinking.

It can perform the tedious transformations required **before the human gets to think**.

That allows a person to spend less time:

* searching
* calculating
* translating
* normalising
* plotting
* formatting
* retrieving
* preparing

…and more time:

* noticing
* wondering
* comparing
* hypothesising
* judging
* asking better questions
* changing direction
* having ideas

This distinction is essential.

The presentation should gradually lead the audience toward it rather than stating it immediately.

---

# 3. THE PHILOSOPHICAL HEART OF THE TALK

One passage should become the emotional and intellectual centre of the presentation.

In 1957, Licklider conducted an informal time-and-motion study of his own technical work.

He concluded that approximately **85% of his “thinking” time was actually spent getting himself into a position where he could think**: retrieving information, calculating, transforming data, plotting graphs and doing other preparatory work.

He describes spending hours producing graphs and then writes:

> “When the graphs were finished, the relations were obvious at once…”

The point is not that graphs are unnecessary.

The point is the opposite.

**The graph enabled the insight.**

But the intellectually valuable event was looking at the graph and realising something.

Hours of clerical effort existed between the question and that moment.

The symbiotic computer collapses that interval.

Later in the paper Licklider explicitly imagines the computer transforming data and plotting graphs in the way requested by the human — including trying multiple alternatives when the human does not yet know exactly what representation they want.

This should be one of the strongest sequences in the entire presentation.

---

# 4. IMPORTANT TONAL RULE

Do not take the presentation too seriously.

The underlying ideas should be taken very seriously.

The presentation itself should have humour, curiosity, surprises and small moments of theatrical absurdity.

Think:

**serious ideas + playful delivery**

rather than:

**serious ideas + serious delivery**

Use occasional deadpan captions such as:

> ELECTRONIC THINKING COMPANION
> MODEL: UNAVAILABLE

or:

> PREPARING TO THINK…
> PLEASE STAND BY

or:

> SEVERAL HOURS LATER

or:

> RESULT:
> “…oh.”

The humour should arise from juxtaposition.

Avoid memes pasted onto slides.

---

# 5. AUDIENCE PARTICIPATION

This should not be a presentation where the audience silently waits until the Q&A.

Build audience participation into the narrative.

There should be approximately **three meaningful checkpoints**.

Questions should make the audience participate in Licklider's argument.

Do not use audience interaction merely as an icebreaker.

Good examples:

### Early

Ask:

> “What do you think people in 1960 thought computers were for?”

Take several answers.

Then introduce what Licklider wanted instead.

### During the time-and-motion section

Ask:

> “What percentage of your working day do you think you actually spend thinking?”

Allow people to guess.

Then reveal Licklider's ~85% observation.

### Later

Ask:

> **“What is something you do in order to think that you frequently mistake for thinking?”**

Possible answers might include searching, compiling, formatting, plotting, gathering documents, reproducing results, converting formats, getting tools to talk to one another, etc.

Give the audience some time here.

If an interactive polling system is available, this could become a live poll or word cloud.

If no polling system is available, design the slide so a show of hands or shouted answers works equally well.

Do not treat every item below as requiring exactly one slide.

Some should be quick cinematic beats.

Some may need two or three slides.

Aim for roughly **16–22 visual scenes**, rather than 16–22 conventional information slides.

## PROLOGUE — A MESSAGE FROM 1960

Start completely black.

Projector noise / film leader aesthetic if transitions permit it.

Then simple type:

> MARCH 1960

Pause.

Then:

> MAN–COMPUTER SYMBIOSIS

Then:

> J. C. R. LICKLIDER

Potential subtitle:

> AN INSTRUCTIONAL FILM ABOUT A MACHINE
> WHICH DOES NOT YET EXIST

Do not immediately explain anything.

Create intrigue.

# 7. WHAT WAS A COMPUTER FOR?

Show the visual world surrounding computing circa 1960:

* large machines
* operators
* paper output
* punched cards
* batch-processing imagery
* technical rooms
* waiting
* forms
* long printouts

Keep text minimal.

The purpose is not to provide a complete computing history.

It is to establish a mental model:

> **You formulate the problem.
> Someone programs it.
> The machine computes it.
> You receive the result.**

Then break that model.

Licklider describes existing computers as primarily solving **pre-formulated problems**, whereas the kind of thinking he wants help with is often difficult or impossible to formulate completely in advance.

Transition into:

> BUT WHAT IF
> YOU DON'T KNOW THE QUESTION YET?

---

# 8. THE QUESTION

Use the Poincaré passage referenced by Licklider.

The important conceptual turn is:

> The problem is not merely obtaining an answer.

It is determining **what should be asked**.

Licklider describes one major purpose of symbiosis as bringing computers into the **formulative** stages of technical thought, rather than only solving already-formulated problems.

This is one of the first places where the presentation should feel oddly contemporary.

Do not immediately say “this is ChatGPT.”

Let the audience notice the resonance themselves.

---

# 9. ENTER: THE FIG WASP

Licklider begins his discussion of symbiosis using the fig tree and its pollinating insect as an example of two very different organisms whose association becomes mutually productive.

Treat this with disproportionate cinematic seriousness.

Potential sequence:

Film title card:

> SYMBIOSIS

Cut to beautiful archival/botanical image of a fig.

Cut to insect illustration.

Labels appear like a scientific film:

> ORGANISM A

> ORGANISM B

Then:

> ALONE: INCONVENIENT
> TOGETHER: THRIVING

The wording can be playful but do not distort the scientific point.

Audience question:

> “So which one are we?”

Do not answer it.

This metaphor should return at the end.

---

# 10. NOT MAN VERSUS MACHINE

Explain briefly that Licklider distinguishes symbiosis from both:

**machine as mere extension of the human**

and

**machine replacing the human entirely**

His proposal occupies the strange middle territory where two systems with very different capabilities cooperate tightly.

Visually this could be a three-part classification card:

> TOOL

> REPLACEMENT

> PARTNER

The third word arrives last.

---

# 11. EXPERIMENT: ONE MAN OBSERVES HIMSELF THINKING

Introduce Licklider's 1957 self-study as though it were a highly serious scientific experiment.

Title card:

> EXPERIMENT 57-B

or simply:

> A PRELIMINARY AND INFORMAL
> TIME-AND-MOTION ANALYSIS
> OF TECHNICAL THINKING

Then:

> SUBJECT: J. C. R. LICKLIDER

> ACTIVITY UNDER OBSERVATION: THINKING

Perhaps show a clock, notebook, work desk, index cards or archival researcher imagery.

Then ask the audience:

> “What percentage of your working day do you reckon you actually spend thinking?”

Collect guesses.

---

# 12. 85%

Next slide:

Nothing but:

# 85%

Wait.

Then introduce the idea:

Most of what Licklider classified as “thinking time” was actually preparation for thought.

Searching.

Calculating.

Plotting.

Transforming.

Retrieving.

Making things comparable.

Preparing the conditions in which a judgement could finally occur.

Do not clutter this slide.

# 13. THE GRAPH — PART I

This sequence should be theatrical.

Present raw-looking experimental data.

It need not reproduce Licklider's actual historical dataset unless reliable data can be found.

If using illustrative data, clearly label it as **ILLUSTRATIVE RECONSTRUCTION**.

Make it intentionally painful to interpret.

Then introduce operations one by one:

> FIND DATA

> CONVERT

> NORMALISE

> CALCULATE

> TRANSFORM

> PLOT

Allow these activities to visually accumulate.

Graph paper.

Rulers.

Pencil marks.

Stacks of calculations.

Film caption:

> SEVERAL HOURS LATER

---

# 14. THE GRAPH — PART II

Suddenly remove the clutter.

Reveal a beautiful, clean graph.

The relationship should be visually obvious.

No explanatory bullet points.

Pause.

Then only:

> “…oh.”

This should get a laugh.

Then reveal the Licklider line:

> “When the graphs were finished, the relations were obvious at once…”

Use only the relevant short excerpt.

Then explain verbally:

The graph did not eliminate thinking.

**It created the object that allowed the interesting thinking to happen.**

---

# 15. THE MANIFESTO SLIDE

Make this visually striking and nearly empty.

# AUTOMATING THE PLOTTING

# IS NOT AUTOMATING THE THINKING.

This is our interpretation of the paper, not a direct Licklider quotation.

Treat it as such.

Then perhaps a second beat:

# AUTOMATE THE DISTANCE

# BETWEEN INTERESTING THOUGHTS.

This line can become a recurring motif throughout the rest of the deck.

---

# 16. INTRODUCE THE SYMBIOTE

From here onward, personify Licklider's imagined computer slightly.

Not as a humanoid robot.

Not as an omniscient AI.

Not as HAL.

Instead imagine a fictional 1960s research product:

> SYMBIOTE™
> ELECTRONIC RESEARCH COMPANION

Potential visual:

a terminal / oscilloscope / desk console with typography and indicators.

The Symbiote should occasionally communicate through small cards.

Examples:

> WOULD YOU LIKE TO SEE THAT ANOTHER WAY?

> THESE MEASUREMENTS USE DIFFERENT DEFINITIONS.

> I HAVE PLOTTED THREE ALTERNATIVES.

> THIS RESULT APPEARS UNUSUAL.

> WHAT HAPPENS IF THIS ASSUMPTION IS REMOVED?

The Symbiote should **present things to think about**, rather than simply announcing answers.

That distinction matters enormously.

# 18. WHO DOES WHAT?

Now introduce Licklider's proposed division of labour.

Humans contribute things like:

* goals
* motivation
* hypotheses
* questions
* models
* approximate judgements
* criteria
* evaluation
* handling unusual situations

Machines contribute things like:

* transformations
* simulations
* calculations
* graph plotting
* interpolation/extrapolation
* testing models
* displaying results
* routinizable operations between decisions

Licklider explicitly describes this complementary relationship.

Do not create a normal comparison table.

Instead make it look like an old **operating manual / personnel assignment diagram**.

Maybe:

> HUMAN DEPARTMENT

versus

> ELECTRONIC DEPARTMENT

Then finally:

> JOINT OPERATIONS

Visually allow the two columns to begin crossing into one another.

---

# 19. THE MACHINE THAT DID NOT EXIST YET

Now let the presentation become slightly fantastical.

Licklider starts listing what would need to exist for real symbiosis.

Treat these as **classified engineering problems** or **future technology dossiers**.

Potential cards:

### FILE 01

TIME SHARING

### FILE 02

MEMORY

### FILE 03

INFORMATION RETRIEVAL

### FILE 04

LANGUAGE

### FILE 05

INPUT / OUTPUT

The paper identifies time-sharing, memory, memory organisation, languages and input/output as prerequisites.

Use authentic diagrams/photos wherever useful.

---

# 20. THE DESK OF THE FUTURE

This should be one of the most delightful sections.

Licklider imagines humans and computers drawing graphs, pictures, notes and equations to each other **on the same display surface**.

He imagines the human sketching a rough graph and the computer understanding it.

He discusses handwriting recognition, direct manipulation of symbols, correcting machine data, sketching table formats and interacting with the machine somewhat like another engineer.

Show these ideas one at a time.

Do not write:

> “HE INVENTED THE IPAD!!!”

Let the audience experience the resemblance themselves.

Possible presentation rhythm:

> DRAW ON THE SCREEN.

cut

> COMPUTER READS YOUR WRITING.

cut

> COMPUTER CLEANS UP THE TABLE.

cut

> HUMAN CORRECTS COMPUTER.

cut

> BOTH USE THE SAME SURFACE.

Then a tiny deadpan title:

> 1960.

---

# 21. TALKING TO THE MACHINE

Licklider also discusses automatic speech production and recognition as possible mechanisms for real-time interaction.

Give this a brief, playful treatment.

Maybe a fake microphone test.

> COMPUTER, CAN YOU HEAR ME?

Static.

> PLEASE SPEAK CLEARLY.

Don't turn this into a history of speech recognition.

It is a flavourful example of how seriously Licklider was considering the physical interaction between human and machine.

---

# 22. THE THINKING CENTRE

Licklider imagines future “thinking centers” combining aspects of libraries with computer information storage, retrieval and symbiotic functionality, with such centres eventually forming a network connected by wide-band communication lines.

This is worth showing visually.

Start with one glowing node.

Then another.

Then another.

Connect them.

Do **not** turn this into the ARPANET presentation.

That is another story.

The purpose here is only to show that his imagined symbiosis naturally begins expanding beyond one person and one machine.

Then move on.

# 23. HITCHHIKER INTERLUDE

# DON'T PANIC.

Then:

> THE COMPUTER IS NOT THE ANSWER.

Next beat:

> IT MIGHT HELP YOU DISCOVER THE QUESTION.

This is not a quotation from Licklider.

Design it like an absurd interstellar field guide entry that has somehow accidentally found its way into a 1960 research film.

Keep it brief.

# 24. BRING IT QUIETLY INTO THE PRESENT

Only now should modern systems enter the presentation.

Do not turn the final third into:

> “…and therefore generative AI!”

Keep the connections broad.

Possible contemporary examples:

* data visualisation
* notebooks
* search engines
* spreadsheets
* debuggers
* IDEs
* simulation tools
* interactive scientific computing
* autocomplete
* coding assistants
* conversational systems
* modern AI tools

The interesting question is not:

> “Can AI do my work?”

It is:

> **“Where is the friction between one interesting thought and the next?”**

and:

> **“Which parts of that friction actually deserve to exist?”**

# 25. AUDIENCE CHECKPOINT

# WHAT DO YOU DO

# IN ORDER TO THINK

# THAT YOU FREQUENTLY

# 26. SYMBIOSIS OR DEPENDENCY?

> TOOL

# 29. VISUAL SYSTEM

Develop an actual design system rather than styling each slide independently.

## Format

Prefer a modern 16:9 deck presented as though a **4:3 piece of archival film is being projected inside it**.

Occasionally allow imagery or typography to escape the film frame for emphasis.

Alternative: use full-frame 4:3 if the presentation environment handles it well.

## Palette

Use a restrained, aged palette:

* warm off-white paper
* charcoal / near-black
* faded tobacco brown
* projector amber
* muted institutional green
* very occasional faded cyan or warning red

Avoid neon “cyberpunk”.

Avoid generic blue technology gradients.

## Texture

Use:

* subtle film grain
* dust
* scratches
* paper fibres
* photocopy artifacts
* imperfect registration
* light halation
* projector vignetting
* typewriter imperfections

These should be restrained enough that text remains highly legible.

## Typography

Use a combination of:

* strong mid-century grotesk/sans-serif
* typewriter or monospace for machine text
* serious editorial serif for quotations/research material
* condensed institutional lettering for chapter cards

Use fonts actually available in the presentation environment.

Do not depend on obscure fonts that will break when the deck moves machines.

## Layout

Avoid normal PowerPoint conventions.

Very few bullets.

Use:

* enormous single numbers
* one-line statements
* diagrams
* photographs
* archival documents
* labels
* captions
* cinematic sequencing
* negative space

A slide is allowed to contain only one word if that is the strongest way to pace the story.

# 30. TRANSITIONS AND MOTION

Transitions should make the deck feel like a film without becoming gimmicky.

Create a small vocabulary and reuse it.

Possible transition motifs:

### Reel change

Brief black frame + chapter title.

### Projector cut

Hard cut with one frame of white / overexposure.

### Typewriter

Text appearing in short mechanical bursts.

### Film splice

Abrupt cut between two related concepts.

### Diagram build

Reveal systems component by component.

### Graph reveal

Messy process disappears and resolves into the finished graph.

### Node expansion

For the thinking-centre/network sequence.

### Slide jam

Very occasionally create a tiny visual “projector malfunction” for humour.

Do not use a different transition for every slide.

Consistency is important.

# 33. WEB RESEARCH FALLBACK: USE PLAYWRIGHT

Normal web search may be blocked or unreliable in the execution environment.

If search tools do not work, **use Playwright to operate a browser directly**.

Do not simply give up because a search API fails.

Suggested research workflow:

1. Open a browser using Playwright.

2. Navigate to a normal web search engine or directly to likely archival sites.

3. Search visually for the required subject.

4. Open promising results.

5. Navigate to the **original asset page**, not merely the image-search thumbnail.

6. Identify the largest useful image version or direct media URL.

7. If direct HTTP/web fetch works, download the asset using the source image URL.

8. If a site blocks direct download, use the browser to reach the original image/media page and obtain the asset through the browser where possible.

9. Never use a screenshot of a search-results page as the actual presentation asset.

10. Record where every historical asset came from.

Potential sources to investigate include:

* Wikimedia Commons
* Internet Archive
* NASA archives
* Library of Congress
* MIT archives and MIT-hosted pages
* Computer History Museum
* Bell Labs / AT&T archival material where accessible
* university archives
* museum collections
* public-domain government archives

Prefer public-domain or openly licensed material where practical.

If licensing information is available, record it.

For every downloaded image maintain an asset log containing:

* local filename
* original source page
* direct asset URL if applicable
* creator/collection if known
* licence/usage note if known
* intended slide

# 34. IMPORTANT RESEARCH RULES

Never fabricate a quote.

Never fabricate a Licklider prediction.

Never attribute one of our modern interpretations directly to Licklider.

Distinguish visually between:

### ORIGINAL TEXT

### PARAPHRASE

### OUR INTERPRETATION

### ILLUSTRATIVE RECONSTRUCTION

Whenever using a quotation, verify it directly against the primary paper.

The presentation should be playful.

The scholarship should be careful.

---

# 35. DO NOT OVERDO “HE PREDICTED THE FUTURE”

The paper contains ideas that feel remarkably familiar today.

That is fun.

But the presentation becomes much less interesting if every section is:

> 1960: LICKLIDER PREDICTS X!

Instead alternate between:

* strange historical context
* philosophical arguments
* specific technical proposals
* things that now feel familiar
* things that remain difficult
* audience reflection
* humour

The goal is to understand **how he was thinking**, not merely tally predictions.

---

# 36. SPEAKER NOTES

Write useful speaker notes for every substantial scene.

Speaker notes should:

* explain what the slide is doing
* give the relevant argument from the paper
* identify whether text is quoted or paraphrased
* provide optional jokes or deadpan remarks
* mark audience questions
* mark intended pauses
* mention transitions
* include citations/source URLs for factual claims
* suggest material that can be skipped if time is short

Do not write a word-for-word speech unless a particular transition benefits from one.

The presenter should have room to improvise.

---

# 37. SOURCE NOTES

Include a final hidden/reference section or speaker-note appendix containing:

* paper citation
* asset citations
* quotation locations
* archival sources
* further reading

Keep citation clutter off the main visual slides unless attribution is important to the visual itself.

---

# 38. FINAL QUALITY TEST

Before considering the presentation complete, inspect it as a continuous experience.

Ask:

**Does this look like someone chose a retro template, or like someone directed a film?**

**Could I remove half the words and make the presentation stronger?**

**Are we explaining Licklider's ideas, or merely admiring his predictions?**

**Does the graph sequence actually make the audience experience the argument?**

**Is audience participation part of the intellectual structure rather than decoration?**

**Does the presentation remain mostly about Man–Computer Symbiosis?**

**Are historical images authentic and sourced?**

**Are any modern interpretations accidentally presented as Licklider quotations?**

**Does the humour feel deadpan and human rather than meme-driven?**

**Does every major visual have a reason to exist?**

If the answer to any is no, revise.
