Scene 00 — The projector starts

Purpose: Establish a screening before the audience knows there is a talk.

Delivery: Say nothing. Press once when the room is settled, let the damaged leader run, then hold on black before advancing.

Sound: The first gesture arms the browser audio context and begins the local procedural projector, splice, and countdown track.

Transition: The countdown deliberately stops at six. Advance from the final black frame directly into the fig.

Source: The leader and fictional laboratory card are original reconstructions, not archival footage.

Scene 01 — A fig

Purpose: Begin the subject with beauty and productive confusion, not explanation.

Delivery: Hold before saying, “I want to begin with a computer scientist talking about…” Pause again. “…figs.” Let the room react.

Motion: The image performs a very slow optical push while the slide remains otherwise empty.

Transition: Cut, rather than dissolve, to the insect specimen.

Source: “Cut Fig 01,” Jedesto, 2021, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Scene 02 — A wasp

Purpose: Use the paper’s own strange biological opening rather than treating it as a disposable metaphor.

Delivery: Explain that the insect pollinates the fig and that the two depend on one another. Avoid introducing computers until the end.

Transition: Use the word “symbiosis” only at the end, then advance into the definition.

Scene 03 — SYMBIOSIS

Purpose: Make the biological idea physical before making the first conceptual reveal.

Delivery: Define symbiosis in ordinary language. Emphasise “dissimilar.” Then ask what happens if one organism is us and the other a computer.

Builds: Type the term, type the definition, couple the physical frames, then reveal the computer sentence only after it has been spoken.

Transition: The final sentence cuts to black. Do not speak over the title card that follows.

Source: Opening argument closely paraphrases Licklider’s first two paragraphs. Images are registered in `SOURCES.md`.

Scene 05 — THE COMPUTER, 1960

Purpose: Establish the long, mediated batch process that Licklider wanted to replace with close interaction.

Delivery: Formulate today, explain tomorrow, compute later, receive mountains of output, then discover the next experiment. Let the paper strip become excessive before landing the final card.

Builds: Six distinct montage beats. Each is a real narrative step, not decorative motion.

Transition: Printer and machine noise stop instantly on the cut to the question.

Source: Process closely paraphrases §2 of the paper. NASA/NACA and U.S. Census archival images are public domain; full records appear in `SOURCES.md`.

Scene 06 — WHAT IS THE QUESTION?

Purpose: Make the first philosophical turn: computers should help formulate problems, not only solve already formulated ones.

Delivery: Let the machine room vanish. Hold the sentence unchanged while explaining the distinction, then name problem formulation as one of Licklider’s two central aims.

Motion: No progressive builds and no ornamental animation. The text holds long enough to change meaning.

Transition: Move into Licklider’s self-study: he investigated what kept him from reaching good questions.

Source: Licklider quotes Poincaré—“The question is not, ‘What is the answer?’ The question is, ‘What is the question?’”—in §2.

Scene 07 — FIELD STUDY: ONE THINKING HUMAN

Purpose: Introduce Licklider’s informal self-observation with deadpan laboratory seriousness.

Delivery: In spring and summer 1957 he tried to track what one “moderately technical person” actually did during work. Reveal that he served as his own subject only after the card has established mock formality.

Builds: Subject dossier; activity/year fields; investigator stamp. Optional line after the last build: “Peer review was unusually convenient.”

Scholarship: He explicitly acknowledges inadequate sampling and hopes his activity spectrum is not typical.

Source: Licklider, §3.1, first two paragraphs.

Scene 08 — Audience experiment #1

Purpose: Turn the audience into Licklider’s next experimental group.

Prompt: “Of the time you call thinking at work, how much do you reckon is actually spent thinking?” Take shouted estimates or hands. Do not reveal Licklider’s result yet.

Interaction: Drag or arrow the range and press “Mark Guess” to leave a grease-pencil tick. The scene works fully without touching the control.

Timing: Take three or four estimates, not a statistically respectable sample.

Source: Audience prompt inspired by Licklider’s self-study in §3.1.

Scene 09 — 85%

Purpose: Reveal the proportion of Licklider’s “thinking” time spent preparing to think.

Delivery: Let the number land. Then name finding information, calculating, plotting, transforming, and preparing the way for a decision or insight.

Scholarship: The figure describes one informal and self-acknowledged inadequate sample. Do not generalise it into a modern workplace statistic.

Transition: “One example involved six experiments and one graph.”

Source: “About 85 per cent of my ‘thinking’ time…” in Licklider, §3.1.

Scene 10 — THE GRAPH, PART I

Purpose: Make the six incompatible speech-intelligibility datasets physically and cognitively messy.

Delivery: Licklider needed to compare six experimental determinations relating speech intelligibility to speech-to-noise ratio. No two experimenters had used the same definition or measure.

Builds: Circle the six measures, identify the definition mismatch, then stamp the obvious verdict: not comparable.

Scholarship: The paper slips and numerical values are illustrative reconstructions. They stage the incompatibility but are not Licklider’s historical data.

Source: Licklider, §3.1.

Scene 11 — THE GRAPH, PART II

Purpose: Make the audience experience clerical delay instead of merely hearing that it occurred.

Delivery: Advance once to start the automatic 12-second sequence. Let `CONVERT → NORMALISE → CALCULATE → PLOT` run without filling the silence. Optional line: “This is the interactive portion.”

Interaction: Further advances can step manually. Reduced-motion mode completes the same deterministic states quickly.

Scholarship: The calculations are illustrative stage mechanics. The several-hour duration and incompatibility are from the paper; these exact values are not.

Source: Licklider, §3.1.

Scene 12 — THE GRAPH, PART III

Purpose: Deliver the immediate visual insight at the emotional centre of the film.

Delivery: Cut here. The clerical rhythm stops before the picture appears. Look at the graph with the audience. Pause. “…right.” Only then explain that it took seconds to determine what Licklider needed to know once the data were comparable.

Screen: No thesis, quotation, title, or explanatory paragraph. The axes contain only what is needed to read the relationship.

Scholarship: This graph is an illustrative reconstruction, not Licklider’s historical plot.

Source: “When the graphs were finished, the relations were obvious at once…” and the six-experiment account in §3.1.

Scene 15 — Human / Symbiote

Purpose: Stage the bidirectional division of labour between unlike capabilities without turning it into a bullet slide.

Delivery: Human cards first: goals, hypotheses, questions, judgement. Send a question across. Reveal the routinizable machine operations, then return alternatives for human evaluation.

Point: The categories are exchanged as physical work cards because this is a partnership loop, not a hierarchy chart.

Source: Licklider, §§3–4, especially humans setting goals and evaluating while machines perform routinizable operations between decisions.

Scene 16 — I don’t know what graph I want

Purpose: Show exploration through alternative representations, not only requested automation.

Delivery: Print the three sheets separately. Let the first two feel merely competent. On the third, reveal the relation nobody explicitly requested.

    Purpose: Reconstruct Licklider’s shared drawing surface literally, without modern application icons.

    Delivery: The human rough mark always comes first. Let the machine trace what it thinks it sees, convert it to precise structure, then ask for judgement rather than silently deciding.

    Purpose: Acknowledge familiar technologies and immediately reject prediction as the thesis.

    Delivery: Advance the six forecast cards faster and faster: handwriting, shared display, speech, retrieval, networks, graphs. Do not explain each item. Interrupt the rhythm with the physical stamp.

    Scene 22 — The actual strange idea

    Purpose: Return to the opening metaphor with earned meaning.

    Delivery: Let the fig and wasp register before replacing them with a human hand and machine circuitry. Then reject mere extension and automation in which the human is a leftover component.

    Point: Licklider proposes a partnership of different capabilities, not a stronger tool on one side or a nearly complete automatic system on the other.

    Source: Licklider, §1. The hand, circuit, labels, and comparison are original educational-film reconstructions.

    Scene 23 — Audience experiment #3

    Purpose: Create the richest discussion checkpoint.

    Prompt: “Which relationship do you actually want with a computer: tool, servant, or partner?” Use hands, movement, spoken answers, or the cards. Do not imply that partner is the correct answer.

    Follow-up: “And what would have to be true for you to call it a partner?” Let two or three answers breathe.

    Interaction: Selecting a card shifts only that local card and reveals the second question. No votes are counted, stored, or transmitted.

    Source: Presentation discussion prompt grounded in Licklider’s distinction in §1.

    Scene 24 — The uncomfortable frame

    Purpose: State the presentation thesis only after the film has earned it.

    Delivery: Let `QUESTION → MACHINE → ANSWER` look seductively complete. Advance once for the grease-pencil refusal, then replace it with the open chain.

    Line: “Symbiosis is not primarily shortening question to answer. It shortens the clerical distance between question, observation, and the next question.”

    Source status: This model is presentation interpretation grounded in Licklider’s formulative aims; it is not a direct quotation.

    Scene 25 — Final image

    Purpose: Let the audience supply the ending.

    Delivery: Return to the exact opening fig and hold. Advance once only when the room is quiet; let the wasp enter slowly. Ask, “What would you do with your 85%?”
