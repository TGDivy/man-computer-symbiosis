Scene 01 — A Message from 1960

Purpose: Begin as a screening, not a lecture. Let the black frame and date establish mystery before the title arrives.

Scholarship: The title, author, journal, volume, and March 1960 date are original bibliographic facts from the paper.

Delivery: Wait through the first beat. Advance once for the title, again for the author. Optional line: “We found this reel in a filing cabinet marked ‘eventually.’”

Transition: Slow projector fade into an abrupt archival cut.

Source: J. C. R. Licklider, “Man-Computer Symbiosis,” IRE Transactions on Human Factors in Electronics, HFE-1, 4–11, March 1960.

Short version: Do not skip; this establishes the film grammar.

Scene 02 — What Was a Computer For?

Purpose: Make the audience articulate the period mental model before Licklider breaks it. This is intellectual participation, not an icebreaker.

Delivery: Ask the question exactly as shown. Take roughly three answers: calculation, military work, payroll, census, science. Avoid correcting anyone. Advance to release the deadpan line.

Scholarship: The photographs are authentic government/archive images, not generated reconstructions. They establish batch-oriented, operator-mediated computing without claiming every 1960 computer worked identically.

Transition: Film splice into the simplified batch model.

Sources: NASA Langley IBM 704 operations, 1957; U.S. Census Bureau keypunch and computer-frame photographs. Full links and licenses are in the reference panel.

Short version: Take one answer, then advance.

Scene 03 — The Batch Model

Purpose: Compress the existing model into one legible mechanism, then break it.

Scholarship: Paraphrase of §2: present-day computers primarily solve preformulated problems or follow predetermined procedures; unforeseen alternatives stop the process.

Delivery: Read the four stages quickly, almost bureaucratically. Advance and pause on the question. Optional line: “The machine is very fast. The queue is philosophical.”

Transition: One-frame overexposure into the Poincaré passage.

Source: Primary paper, §2 “Aims of Man-Computer Symbiosis.”

Short version: Keep the final question; the pipeline can be covered in one sentence.

Scene 04 — The Question

Purpose: Make the conceptual turn from obtaining answers to shaping questions.

Scholarship: The Poincaré line appears verbatim in Licklider’s §2. Attribute it as a quotation used by Licklider, not as Licklider’s own sentence. Licklider’s explicit aim is to bring computing into the “formulative parts” of technical problems.

Delivery: Let the audience read the quote. Advance to move attention from solution to formulation. Do not mention contemporary chat systems; allow the resonance to emerge.

Transition: A botanical educational-film cut: abstract question to literal symbiosis.

Source: Primary paper, §2.

Short version: Do not skip; this defines the paper’s first main aim.

Scene 05 — Enter: The Fig Wasp

Purpose: Treat Licklider’s opening metaphor with comically excessive seriousness, then establish productive interdependence between dissimilar partners.

Scholarship: §1.1 describes a fig tree and pollinating insect as mutually dependent and uses Webster’s definition of symbiosis: dissimilar organisms living together in close association. The visual pairing is illustrative; it is not presented as a specimen record of the exact taxon printed in the paper.

Delivery: Advance through “alone/together,” then ask “So which one are we?” Do not answer. Optional line: “The film’s entomology department requests that we move along.”

Transition: Botanical labels reorganize into a classification card.

Sources: Primary paper §1.1; 1801 NYPL botanical plate; public-domain Blastophaga illustration. Full links in references.

Short version: Show the pairing and move on after the unanswered question.

Scene 06 — Not Man Versus Machine

Purpose: Position symbiosis between a mere mechanical extension and full replacement.

Scholarship: §1.2 explicitly distinguishes symbiosis from “mechanically extended man” and from systems where people remain only because some functions could not be automated. “Tool / replacement / partner” is our compressed classification, not Licklider’s exact wording.

Delivery: Explain A and B in one sentence each. Advance to “partner.” Stress that dissimilarity is the source of supplementation, not a defect.

Transition: Reel-change blackout into the time-and-motion experiment.

Source: Primary paper, §§1.2 and 3.1.

Short version: Keep only the three-way distinction.

Scene 07 — Experiment 57-B

Purpose: Turn the 1957 self-study into a deadpan scientific reveal and make the audience commit to an estimate.

Scholarship: In spring and summer 1957, Licklider tracked the work of “one moderately technical person,” himself. He repeatedly calls the sample inadequate and the analysis preliminary and informal. “Experiment 57-B” is our theatrical label.

Delivery: Ask for guesses aloud or use the slider. Let several people answer. Do not reveal the number here. Optional line: “The ethics committee consisted of the subject, who had concerns.”

Transition: Hard numerical cut.

Sources: Primary paper §3.1; public-domain Licklider portrait from the U.S. National Library of Medicine collection.

Short version: Take two guesses and advance.

Scene 08 — 85%

Purpose: Make the number land before explaining it. The emotional point is not inefficiency; it is the distance between a question and the conditions for insight.

Scholarship: Licklider writes, “About 85 per cent of my ‘thinking’ time was spent getting into a position to think, to make a decision, to learn something I needed to know.” The surrounding task words are faithful paraphrases from §3.1.

Delivery: Pause on the bare number. Advance for the sentence, then again for the orbiting tasks. Resist saying “wasted”; Licklider says these operations were necessary.

Transition: Mechanical accumulation into the graph-making sequence.

Source: Primary paper §3.1.

Short version: Do not skip; this is the talk’s central evidence.

Scene 09 — The Graph, Part I

Purpose: Let the audience feel the clerical barrier instead of merely hearing about it.

Scholarship: Licklider describes comparing six determinations of speech intelligibility versus speech-to-noise ratio; no two experimenters used the same definition or measure. The values shown here are invented and clearly labelled “illustrative reconstruction.”

Delivery: Advance through the operations at a steady, slightly oppressive rhythm. The repeated stamps are the joke. Let “Several hours later” sit for a beat.

Transition: The graph-paper clutter resolves instantly into one clean display.

Source: Primary paper §3.1. No claim is made that these were Licklider’s historical data.

Short version: Advance quickly through all six operations; preserve the contrast with the next scene.

Scene 10 — The Graph, Part II

Purpose: Create the experience of immediate insight. The graph is not decoration; it is the object that makes the relationship perceptible.

Scholarship: The plot is an illustrative reconstruction, not Licklider’s dataset. The short quotation is verbatim from §3.1. He continues that plotting “had to be done” to make the relation obvious.

Delivery: Say nothing at first. Let people inspect the curve. Advance to “…oh.” Pause for the laugh, then reveal the quote. Explain verbally: the graph did not eliminate thinking; it enabled the interesting thinking.

Transition: The curve becomes a stark manifesto.

Source: Primary paper §3.1.

Short version: Never rush the first silent beat.

Scene 12 — Introducing the Symbiote

Purpose: Personify the imagined computer without making it humanoid, omniscient, or an answer machine.

Scholarship: SYMBIOTE™ is fictional. Its messages paraphrase §4: the equipment tests models, transforms data, plots alternatives when the human is unsure, displays results, and performs routinizable work between decisions.

Delivery: Advance through the messages as examples of a system presenting objects for judgement. Optional line: “Accessories include a precise draftsman and absolutely no face.”

Transition: The console’s linear process diagram is stamped insufficient.

Source: Primary paper §4.

Short version: Show only the first and third messages.

Scene 13 — Not Question → Answer

Purpose: Replace the default input/output story with an iterative loop of representation and judgement.

Scholarship: The diagram is our interpretation, not Licklider’s. It combines his formulative aim (§2), graph example (§3.1), and division of labour (§4).

Delivery: Present the first diagram as deliberately boring. Advance for the stamp, then build the loop. Emphasize that the machine creates something the person can inspect, causing a better question.

Transition: Loop nodes become departmental assignments.

Source: Primary paper §§2–4.

Scene 14 — Who Does What?

Purpose: Show complementarity as an operating assignment, then undermine any too-neat division with joint operations.

Scholarship: The lists closely paraphrase §4. Licklider assigns people goals, hypotheses, questions, criteria, evaluations, and low-probability situations; equipment converts and tests models, simulates, transforms, plots, interpolates, and handles routinizable clerical operations.

Delivery: Do not read every item. Point out two or three on each side, then advance. Licklider says the contributions may blend so completely they are hard to separate.

Transition: Manual page folds into classified future-technology dossiers.

Source: Primary paper §4.

Short version: Name one human contribution, one machine contribution, and reveal “joint operations.”

Scene 16 — The Desk of the Future

Purpose: Let the audience encounter the physical intimacy Licklider imagined, one capability at a time.

Scholarship: §5.5.1 explicitly proposes a shared display surface for graphs, pictures, notes, and equations; rough graph input; handwriting recognition; symbol definition; table cleanup; data correction; flow diagrams; and interaction resembling work with another engineer.

Delivery: Build the five actions. You may draw a rough curve on the surface and press “Clean this up.” The interaction is illustrative, not a claim about a 1960 prototype. Let the tiny “1960.” do the work; avoid “he invented the iPad.”

Transition: Desk console microphone test.

Source: Primary paper §5.5.1.

Short version: Show the first, third, and fifth actions; demonstration optional.

Scene 17 — Talking to the Machine

Purpose: Add a brief physical-interaction example without turning the talk into a speech-recognition history.

Scholarship: §5.5.3 discusses speech production and recognition, notes 98% recognition of spoken decimal digits, and estimates that symbiotic interaction might require about 2,000 words under constrained speaking conditions.

Delivery: Use the fake microphone test if desired; it requests no microphone access. Advance for the 2,000-word specification. Optional line: “Model currently fluent in digits and optimism.”

Transition: The oscilloscope glow expands into a node on a network.

Sources: Primary paper §5.5.3; CC BY 2.0 DuMont oscilloscope photograph by Tomislav Medak.

Short version: One sentence and the specification card.

Scene 18 — The Thinking Centre

Purpose: Briefly expand symbiosis from one desk to shared institutions and a network, then stop before the talk becomes networking history.

Scholarship: §5.1 imagines, 10 or 15 years ahead, a “thinking center” combining libraries, information storage and retrieval, and symbiotic functions, then a network of such centres connected by wide-band lines.

Delivery: Build one node, then the network. Say explicitly: “That is another reel.” The historical operations-room image illustrates shared-display context; it is not a photograph of Licklider’s proposed thinking centre.

Transition: Network collapses into a cosmic field-guide interruption.

Sources: Primary paper §5.1; public-domain NORAD operations-room booklet image, c.1965.

Short version: Show one node and the network; omit the image discussion.

Scene 20 — The Friction Now

Purpose: Bring the paper into the present without turning the final act into an AI product pitch.

Scholarship: Contemporary tool names and both questions are our interpretation. The framing follows Licklider’s concern with routinizable operations between decisions and insights.

Delivery: Name a few tools, not all. Track the corridor from thought 01 through friction to thought 02. Advance and distinguish task replacement from interval compression.

Transition: Remove everything except the audience’s own friction.

Scene 21 — What Do You Mistake for Thinking?

Purpose: Make the audience apply Licklider’s self-study to their own work. This is the most important participation pause.

Scholarship: The question is ours, derived from Licklider’s distinction between thinking and the clerical preparation that occupied most of his “thinking” time.

Delivery: Read the question and wait. Take shouted answers; type a few into the local word field if useful. Nothing is transmitted or stored beyond this browser. Advance only after the room has had time. Then ask the two follow-ups without forcing a consensus.

Transition: Audience words drift into the final human/machine pairing.

Source: Interpretation grounded in primary paper §3.1.

Scene 22 — Compress the Distance

Purpose: Return to the fig metaphor and end on productive difference, not human-versus-machine competition.

Scholarship: The capability words paraphrase §§3.1–4. The final statement is our interpretation, clearly labelled, not a Licklider quote.

Delivery: Recall that productive partners need not be alike. Advance for the intermingled capabilities, then the final statement. Let it sit. Advance once more: the projector stops, and the post-credit card appears after a delay. Do not narrate the sequel list unless invited.

Closing option: “Tomorrow, notice one tedious operation between two interesting questions. Licklider would automate that bit.”

Transition: Black. Projector stops. Post-credit card.
