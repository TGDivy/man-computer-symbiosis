# Speaker Notes

These are the full rehearsal notes for the implemented 32-scene deck, Scene 00 through Scene 31. The notes panel opened with **N** contains a compact version of the same argument; this document is the preparation copy, with source locations, delivery language, transitions, timing, and quote status.

## Global delivery

- Do not name the paper before Scene 04. The audience should encounter the biological relation before learning that this is a computing talk.
- Treat the film language seriously. Do not apologize for the leader, archive cards, or SYMBIOTE joke.
- The central argument is a loop, not a division chart: people set direction and evaluate; computers perform the routinizable preparation; what returns changes the next human question.
- Let the graph sequence take time. Scene 12 should feel longer than is comfortable; Scene 13 should feel immediate.
- The prerequisite reel is causal: availability during thought → reachable memory → goals rather than fixed routes → a shared surface → natural input/output → the question of what relation those capabilities enable.
- Scenes 18 and 27 are discussions, not surveys. No response is persisted, exported, transmitted, or presented as consensus.
- “Golden age of human creativity” is our interpretation. Licklider's exact §1.2 phrase is “the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind.” Keep that distinction audible.
- Start with sound on if the room permits it. The score is procedural and responds to scenes and builds: the organic motif yields to clerical pressure, the product-film rhythm, accumulating prerequisite voices, interlocking relationship motifs, and a warm coda. Silence and subtraction are part of the pacing—especially the Scene 03 question, Scene 13's release, the Scene 19 reel break, and the Scene 29 quotation. Recognition and playback duck the score while speech is active.
- Full running time is roughly 24–28 minutes before discussion. For a shorter cut, preserve Scenes 01–07, 10–16, 19–20, 22–30, and the credits.

## Scene 00 — The projector starts

**Purpose.** Establish a screening before the audience knows there is a presentation.

**Delivery.** Say nothing. Once the room has settled, press Space or click once. Let the archive card and 8–7–6 leader run completely, then hold briefly on black.

**Control and sound.** The first gesture arms browser audio: projector motor, film hiss, splice clicks, and countdown pops. The rewind control at bottom-left restarts this exact leader and sound sequence without reloading.

**Transition.** Cut cleanly from black into the fig. Do not explain the found-film conceit.

**Source status.** Original reconstruction. “Research Film Archive” is fictional.

## Scene 01 — A fig

**Purpose.** Begin where Licklider begins, but make the mood curious and alive rather than eerie.

**Suggested words.** “I want to begin with a scientist talking about…” Pause. “…figs.”

Hold long enough for the green tree and fruit to register. There is deliberately no caption. The audience should first see a living system, not receive a definition.

**Sound and transition.** Let the organic bed arrive almost imperceptibly. Hard-cut—not dissolve—to the insect already inside the relation.

**Source.** Licklider, §1.1; ripe fig photograph attribution in `SOURCES.md`. `?opening=mystery` selects the preserved cut-fig alternate.

## Scene 02 — A wasp

**Purpose.** Make the second organism and the biological dependence concrete before introducing a computer.

**Suggested words.** “Inside this relationship is a tiny pollinating wasp. The wasp reproduces within figs and carries pollen between them. Each species participates in the other's life cycle.”

**Build 1.** Keep the habitat photograph visible while the scientific specimen plate slides in. Let it feel like an older technical film interrupting a nature image.

**Build 2.** Reveal the rule and *Blastophaga psenes* label. The base frame already identifies the habitat photograph as *Pleistodontes*; say that the reference plate shows a separately sourced pollinating fig-wasp species, not the same insect.

**Accuracy.** Do not repeat an on-screen categorical “only” claim. Licklider's opening uses one taxonomic example and period wording; fig–wasp biology contains many species-specific relations. Do not use the parasitoid *Apocrypta* as the mutualist.

**Transition.** End with one word: “Symbiosis.”

**Source.** Licklider, §1.1. Image creators, licences, and species caveat are in `SOURCES.md`.

## Scene 03 — Symbiosis

**Purpose.** Move from Licklider's biological opening to the paper's central question without turning the analogy into a label-swapping trick.

**Build 1.** Let “SYMBIOSIS” type in while the fig and wasp remain separate documentary frames. Do not yet name a human or a computer.

**Build 2.** Reveal the definition. Licklider quotes symbiosis as the “living together in intimate association, or even close union, of two dissimilar organisms.” The slide is a close excerpt; it omits “or even close union” for pace.

**Before Build 3.** “The fig and the wasp give Licklider a model: unlike participants whose association produces something neither produces alone. He then asks what an association between two entirely different participants would require.”

**Build 3.** The photographs and definition disappear completely. Read the black-screen question once: “What would symbiosis between a human and a computer require?” This wording makes both members of the new pair explicit; it avoids the accidental implication in “what if one of them is a computer?” that a fig or wasp remains on the other side.

**Sound and transition.** At Build 3 the organic motif, projector texture, and motor all withdraw. Hold the physically empty black question in complete silence, then advance to the title. Do not speak over the title's first beat.

**Source.** Licklider, §1.1. The definition there is quoted from *Webster's New International Dictionary* (1958); the on-screen sentence is explicitly a close excerpt rather than a complete quotation.

## Scene 04 — Title card, finally

**Purpose.** Name the paper only after its opening analogy has been experienced.

**Delivery.** Hold the title in silence. When the credit appears: “J. C. R. Licklider, 1960—writing before interactive computing existed as an ordinary experience.”

**Sound.** Let the organic bed fall away into the restrained title chord. Do not rush the reel-change mark.

**Transition.** “To understand what he wanted, we need to remember what using a computer meant.”

**Source.** J. C. R. Licklider, “Man-Computer Symbiosis,” *IRE Transactions on Human Factors in Electronics*, HFE-1, March 1960, pp. 4–11.

## Scene 05 — The computer, 1960

**Purpose.** Establish the batch process that makes close cooperation impossible.

**Builds 1–4.** Walk through the delay: formulate the problem today; spend tomorrow with a programmer; wait until next week for the machine; receive the answer later.

**Build 5.** Let the paper feed. Licklider's own example gives the computer five minutes to assemble the program, 47 seconds to calculate, and returns a sheet of paper 20 feet long.

**Build 6.** “And instead of a final solution, the output merely suggests another experiment.” Stop the machine rhythm here.

**Transition.** “The answer has arrived. But the real work is discovering what to ask next.”

**Source.** Close staging of Licklider's battle-planning example in §2. The photographs are archival illustrations, not one continuous historical event.

## Scene 06 — What is the question?

**Purpose.** Turn from solving a preformulated problem to helping formulate one.

**Suggested words.** “Licklider quotes Poincaré: ‘The question is not, “What is the answer?” The question is, “What is the question?”’ A computer that only enters after the problem is completely specified arrives too late for much of the interesting work.”

Hold the unchanged frame while making the distinction. Do not turn this into a generic slogan about curiosity.

**Sound and transition.** The bed drops close to silence. “That gives Licklider the first of two aims.”

**Source.** Exact quotation as reproduced by Licklider in §2.

## Scene 07 — Two aims

**Purpose.** State the paper's two governing aims before the evidence.

**Build 1 — formulate.** “First: bring the computer into the formulative part of technical work, while the important question is still taking shape.”

**Build 2 — think in time.** “Second: bring computation into thought that has to happen in real time, before the situation has moved on.”

If the distinction feels abstract, return briefly to the battle example: a week-long computation cycle cannot participate in a situation changing minute by minute.

**Transition.** “Why should a machine participate in thinking at all? Licklider starts with a rather personal experiment.”

**Source.** Paper summary and §2. Both aims are close paraphrases, not newly invented categories.

## Scene 08 — 1957

**Purpose.** Make the time-and-motion study concrete and appropriately modest.

**Base frame.** Treat the dossier seriously: “The subject was one moderately technical person.” It is present as soon as the scene begins; there is no empty first advance.

**Build 1.** “During the spring and summer of 1957.”

**Build 2.** “The investigator and subject were the same person: Licklider himself. What actually filled the hours he regarded as work?”

Use his caveat: “Although I was aware of the inadequacy of the sampling, I served as my own subject.” This is not a population study.

**Transition.** “The answer was not mostly insight. It was the work required to get near one.”

**Source.** Licklider, §3.1.

## Scene 09 — Getting into position to think

**Purpose.** Give the percentage substantive meaning before showing it.

**Builds.** Accumulate the operations in pairs: search and obtain; calculate and normalize; plot and transform; determine the consequences of assumptions or hypotheses.

**Suggested words.** “These are not pointless tasks. They prepare the way for a decision or insight. But Licklider calls them ‘essentially clerical or mechanical’—and they were determining which intellectual questions were feasible to attempt.”

Do not ask the audience for examples yet; let recognition build toward Scene 18.

**Transition.** “How much of his so-called thinking time did this consume?”

**Source.** Licklider, §3.1, especially the paragraph beginning “Throughout the period I examined…”

## Scene 10 — 85 percent

**Purpose.** Land the finding only after the work behind it is visible.

Hold the number. Then reveal and quote: “About 85 per cent of my ‘thinking’ time was spent getting into a position to think, to make a decision, to learn something I needed to know.”

Let it feel both alarming and slightly funny. Immediately qualify it: this was Licklider's informal estimate of his own time, not a universal productivity statistic.

**Transition.** “One example makes those hours visible.”

**Source.** Exact quotation, Licklider, §3.1.

## Scene 11 — The graph, part one

**Purpose.** Make incompatibility intuitive before any calculation begins.

**Suggested words.** “He needed to compare six experiments about one underlying relationship: speech intelligibility as the speech-to-noise ratio changes. But no two experimenters had used the same definition or measure.”

**Build 1.** Name the shared question.

**Build 2.** Reveal the obstacle: six measurement systems that cannot yet be compared.

**Accuracy.** The six labels and miniature records are educational reconstructions. They communicate the mismatch; they are not Licklider's original worksheets.

**Transition.** “Before he could inspect the relation, every experiment had to be translated into a common form.”

**Source.** Licklider, §3.1.

## Scene 12 — The graph, part two

**Purpose.** Make clerical delay felt in the room.

Advance once and let the automatic sequence run: convert → normalize → calculate → plot. Resist the urge to fill all the silence. An optional dry line is: “This is the interactive portion.” With reduced motion enabled, the sequence does not run automatically; advance Builds 1–5 manually and preserve the same sense of duration through delivery.

The counter and exact arithmetic are stage mechanics. The paper supplies the substantive facts: six incompatible definitions, several hours of calculation, and a comparison that became immediate only afterward.

**Sound.** Mechanical layers and pitch accumulate with each operation. Let them stop completely before Scene 13.

**Transition.** Advance only after “SEVERAL HOURS LATER” has had time to register.

**Source.** Licklider, §3.1.

## Scene 13 — The graph, part three

**Purpose.** Deliver the suddenness of insight once the representation is ready.

Cut to the clean graph. Look at it with the audience. Pause. Say only: “…right.” Then explain: “When the graphs were finished, the relations were obvious at once.”

Follow with the paper's contrast: several hours to make the data comparable; a few seconds to determine what he needed to know.

**Accuracy.** This curve is an illustrative reconstruction, not the historical graph.

**Sound.** Silence. Do not add a revelation sting.

**Source.** Licklider, §3.1.

## Scene 14 — A manifesto

**Purpose.** State the interpretation earned by the graph sequence.

Let the first line stand: “The machine would not have the insight.” Beat. Reveal the second: “It could help us reach a view where human insight could happen.”

Read neither as product copy. The on-screen label marks this as a provisional extrapolation: routinizable preparation could shorten the clerical distance to a representation on which human judgment could act. It is not a claim that a computer produced Licklider's historical graph.

**Quote status.** Both lines are presentation interpretation, not quotations from Licklider.

**Transition.** The seriousness makes room for the straight-faced product film that follows.

**Source.** Interpretive synthesis of §3.1 and the division of work in §4.

## Scene 15 — SYMBIOTE™ enters

**Purpose.** Give the proposed electronic companion a memorable period-film character.

Play this completely straight. Reveal the brand, then the console portrait, then the features as if this were a 1962 institutional sales reel. Do not explain the joke.

**Suggested words.** “Introducing SYMBIOTE: the electronic research companion for problems not yet fully formulated.”

When the capability rail appears, say “returns variants,” not “discovers answers.” That language sets up Scenes 16–17 and keeps judgment with the person.

**Accuracy.** SYMBIOTE, its manufacturer, model number, and claims are fictional. The SAGE console photograph is authentic equipment used illustratively.

**Sound and transition.** A restrained product chord replaces the clerical bed. “A companion only means something if we can describe how work passes between the partners.”

**Source.** Fictional reconstruction based on the functions described in §4.

## Scene 16 — One working exchange

**Purpose.** Show the division of work as movement, not another static two-column conclusion.

**Build 1.** “The person supplies direction: a goal, a hypothesis, a criterion.”

**Build 2.** Send the hypothesis card: “Test this relation.”

**Build 3.** “The computer retrieves, normalizes, transforms, simulates, plots, and compares.”

**Build 4.** Let the return card travel back: evidence, an anomaly, an alternative representation.

**Build 5.** “The person evaluates those returns—and asks a better next question.”

Licklider's §4 list is richer: people also supply motivation, models, approximate leading contributions, and judgment; equipment converts hypotheses into testable models, tests against data, simulates, transforms, interpolates, extrapolates, and displays. The slide is a rehearsal-sized example, not a rigid allocation.

**Transition.** “Sometimes the better next question is not a more precise command. It is a request for alternatives.”

**Source.** Licklider, §4. “Evidence / anomaly / alternative” is presentation synthesis grounded in §§2–4.

## Scene 17 — Show me several cuts

**Purpose.** Demonstrate exploratory support when the human does not yet know which representation will matter.

Print the three plot sheets separately. Let the first two feel merely competent. On the third, reveal the marked possible relation and hold it long enough for “human review required” to register.

**Paper language.** Licklider says the equipment may plot graphs, “cutting the cake” as specified, “or in several alternative ways if the human operator is not sure what he wants.”

Do not describe this as autonomous scientific discovery. The plots and anomaly are illustrative; their value is to offer material for human inspection and a changed question.

**Transition.** “What work do we still perform merely to get ourselves into this position?”

**Source.** Exact phrases from Licklider, §4.

## Scene 18 — Audience experiment two

**Purpose.** Let the room locate its own clerical distance to thought.

Ask: “Name one thing you do in order to think.” Give the room twenty real seconds. Prefer spoken answers; use the field only if it helps make two or three answers visible.

Fallback examples: searching, formatting, compiling, reproducing, plotting, translating representations, preparing a comparison.

The form holds at most three temporary cards. It deliberately has no Copy, CSV, Print, persistence, network, or tally behavior.

**Transition.** Choose one audience answer and ask: “What would a machine need in order to shorten that distance while thought is still happening?”

**Source.** Participation prompt grounded in the self-study in §3.1.

## Scene 19 — The machine that did not yet exist

**Purpose.** Give the prerequisite chapter a reason before presenting its components.

Let the reel splice complete in silence. Then: “Licklider has described the partnership. But the equipment and programs it requires do not yet exist together.”

Use his opening to §5: “There are in fact several hurdles that stand between the nonsymbiotic present and the anticipated symbiotic future.”

**Spoken bridge.** “So what would a computer need in order to be present while thought was still happening?”

**Sound.** This is a reset: product-film sound falls away; the prerequisite bed begins only with Scene 20.

**Source.** Licklider, opening of §5.

## Scene 20 — At the moment of thought

**Purpose.** Answer the real-time aim from Scene 07 and the batch delay from Scene 05.

**Build 1.** Connect several users around one machine. In 1960 a large computer was too fast and too expensive for real-time cooperation with one person; it had to divide its time. The photograph is a dated 1970 illustration, not a 1960 prototype from the paper.

**Build 2.** “Not next week. Not tomorrow. At the moment of thought.” Explain time sharing in that human sense: availability while the question is alive.

**Build 3.** Widen to Licklider's “thinking center,” combining library functions, information storage and retrieval, and symbiotic computation; he even imagines a network of such centers.

**Optional contemporary bridge.** “The machinery has changed, but pooled access to powerful computation is still how many of us reach more machine than one person could own.” Keep this spoken; do not introduce cloud/server-rack imagery.

**Transition.** “Being present is not enough if the machine cannot reach the record of what people already know.”

**Source.** Licklider, §5.1.

## Scene 21 — Published memory

**Purpose.** Make memory and organization concrete through one retrieval action. The scene is not a generic search interface and does not imply that the computer generated an answer.

**Before the first build — paper context.** Licklider first rejects the fantasy that all technical literature will immediately be placed in computer memory. In §5.2 he writes: “Books are among the most beautifully engineered, and human-engineered, components in existence,” and says they will remain functionally important. He hopes the computer will expedite their “finding, delivering, and returning.” Keep this in the spoken account; the card drawer stands for an external record that is kept, organized, and reachable.

**Build 1 — enter by name.** “Now watch his concrete example.” In §5.3, Licklider describes a trie memory entered one character at a time from a standard initial register. Use his argument, “matrix multiplication,” without modernizing it into a prompt.

**Build 2 — retrieve the associated procedure.** His exact claim is that by entering with the argument “matrix multiplication,” one might retrieve “the entire program for performing a matrix multiplication on the computer.” Emphasize the distinction: the stored name leads to an associated procedure available for inspection and use; the machine has not invented a method or supplied a conclusion.

**Build 3 — inspect what is nearby.** Licklider's branching organization shares storage where arguments share initial characters. A person can step backward along that common beginning and inspect what “thus-far similar items” have been stored. His own illustration moves from a missing citation for “Egan, J. P.” back to the trail for “Egan, James … .” The three small cards—shared beginning, nearby names, similar items—are a visual condensation of that mechanism, not a claim about contemporary semantic search.

**Build 4 — name or point.** Read the close excerpt slowly: the desired properties bring computer storage into resonance with people's tendency to designate things “by naming or pointing.” “Pointing” matters because the person need not always arrive with a perfectly formulated symbolic name.

**Transition.** “But finding a procedure is different from telling a machine what you are trying to achieve.”

**Source.** Licklider, §§5.2–5.3. The book sentence, matrix-multiplication example, Egan example, and “naming or pointing” language all come directly from those sections. The photographic card drawer is an archival metaphor; it is not presented as Licklider's proposed trie-memory hardware.

## Scene 22 — Goals, not routes

**Purpose.** Make the language problem the next causal obstacle.

**Base frame.** “Instructions to a person normally express a goal and a criterion for knowing when it has been reached.” This side is visible immediately, so the scene never opens empty.

**Builds 1–2.** Introduce the division, then contrast the goal with a conventional program specifying individual steps and their sequence.

**Build 3.** Connect back to §2: when an unforeseen alternative appears, a predetermined process halts until the program is extended.

Quote the compact line exactly: “Instructions directed to computers specify courses; instructions directed to human beings specify goals.”

Preserve Licklider's two proposed routes through the language problem: problem-solving, hill-climbing, and self-organizing programs; or real-time concatenation of named preprogrammed segments and closed subroutines. He calls the first route promising, but at the time only a “demonstration in principle.” Do not imply that he considered goal-level programming solved.

**Transition.** “If the person begins with a rough goal rather than a complete route, the interface has to accept something rough—and help make it precise.”

**Source.** Licklider, §5.4; unforeseen-alternative framing in §2.

## Scene 23 — The same surface

**Purpose.** Let the goals scene cause the interface scene: rough intention becomes shared, precise material.

**Build 1.** A person sketches a function rapidly, with the flexibility of a pencil and doodle pad.

**Build 2.** The computer recognizes marks and posts its interpretation in precise form.

**Build 3.** Both remain on the same editable surface so the person can inspect, correct, and continue.

**Build 4 — brief fold-in.** Scale the principle from one person to a team. Licklider's wall display provides common, current information while individual stations preserve role-specific detail and control. Common situation does not mean identical responsibilities.

The IBM 2250 image is from 1969 and the NORAD control-room image from 1964. Both dates appear on screen. They are later illustrations, not Licklider prototypes or evidence that his proposed interface had already been realized.

**Transition.** “Drawing was one natural channel. Licklider also asked whether we might simply speak.”

**Source.** Licklider, §5.5.1 (desk-surface display) and §5.5.2 (computer-posted wall display).

## Scene 24 — Speak and listen

**Purpose.** Make one input/output prerequisite tangible rather than presenting another prediction slide.

**Delivery.** Licklider asks both whether speech interaction is desirable and whether it is feasible. He is cautious: production was easier than recognition, and the required vocabulary, diverse speakers, and accents were serious constraints.

Preflight this scene in the exact managed browser, account, network, and room setup. Recognition may be implemented by a browser- or vendor-operated service, so do not promise local-only processing. Coordinate with the CART or live-caption operator before rehearsal so the demo's transcript and synthetic playback are not mistaken for the room's accessibility captions.

Invite one short phrase. Press Record only when ready for the browser permission prompt; inspect the transcript; then play it back. The score ducks while recognition or playback is active. If recognition is unavailable, permission fails, or the prompt takes more than a moment, choose **Use text instead** immediately. Playback can still work independently.

Be explicit that this is a current browser demonstration of the interaction category, not a reconstruction of a 1960 recognizer. The page itself does not store the audio or transcript, but the browser or its service provider may process audio outside the page.

**Transition.** “Time sharing, memory, organization, language, displays, speech—Licklider calls all of these prerequisites.”

**Source.** Licklider, §5.5.3.

## Scene 25 — Prerequisites for realization

**Purpose.** Name the chapter after its logic has accumulated, then move from capabilities to relationship.

Build the ring while briefly pointing backward: availability at the moment of thought; reachable memory; organization for names and patterns; language that can express goals; immediate input and output.

Emphasize the precise paper term: these are **prerequisites**. Their later familiarity does not by itself answer what sort of human–computer system they create.

Do not revive the old slogan “the equipment does not create the partnership.” The paper's distinction is more useful and less preachy.

**Transition.** Ask the screen's question exactly: “What kind of relation do they make possible?”

**Source.** Paper summary and §5.

## Scene 26 — Three relations

**Purpose.** Use Licklider's own categories instead of announcing “the actual strange idea.”

**Build 1 — mechanical extension.** The human supplies initiative, direction, integration, and criterion; apparatus extends an arm or eye.

**Build 2 — humanly extended machine.** Automation is primary and the people who remain handle functions it proved infeasible to automate. Licklider calls this semi-automatic and explicitly says it is not symbiosis.

**Build 3 — symbiotic partnership.** Different capabilities cooperate continuously. Neither side is simply an appendage or leftover.

Do not imply “partner” is automatically the correct design or moral label in every system. The scene gives the room distinctions with which to reason.

**Transition.** “Which of these relationships do we actually want?”

**Source.** Licklider, §1.2.

## Scene 27 — Audience experiment three

**Purpose.** Test the paper's categories against relationships people actually want, without manufacturing a vote.

Name the framing before the prompt: tool, servant, and partner are contemporary discussion vocabulary, not Licklider's three labels. Then ask: “Which relationship do you actually want with a computer: tool, servant, or partner?” Use hands, movement, or spoken answers if possible. The cards are available as theatrical objects, not a data collection system.

Select any card to reveal the real question: “What would have to be true for you to call it a partner?” Let two or three answers breathe. Listen for agency, complementary capability, trust, contestability, shared context, and the ability to alter the next question—but do not supply those answers before the room does.

Nothing is counted, stored, copied, downloaded, or treated as consensus.

**Transition.** “We do not need a hypothetical example. The thing on this screen gives us a small one.”

**Source.** Original discussion prompt grounded in the distinctions in §1.2.

## Scene 28 — Difficult to separate neatly

**Purpose.** Use the making of this presentation as evidence and complicate the clean exchange diagram from Scene 16.

**Opening words.** “This presentation was made through one actual, live exchange of this kind.” Do not introduce the scene as a clean split between “human direction” and “computer preparation.” The point is that authorship, implementation, criticism, and revised questions recur around the same artifact.

**Build 1 — authorship before build.** “Before anything was built, I had to work out the argument, storyboard the sequence, decide how the presentation should move and feel, and define an art direction strong enough to hold it together.” Point to the four cards. These are not cosmetic preferences: each one constrains what counts as a successful scene and what should be rejected.

**Build 2 — computer-assisted iteration.** “Then, while the thought was still live, I could ask the computer to build several variations and make their consequences visible.” It implemented alternatives, captured and tested them, returned failures, and supported parallel critique passes framed around a possible audience reading, historical accuracy, visual coherence, and functional behavior. It also proposed edits and sometimes reframed the question. Those passes are useful perspectives—not real audience testimony, not independent authorities, and not decisions.

**Build 3 — judgment returns to the storyboard.** “I compared what came back, chose, rejected, redirected, and sometimes rebuilt the storyboard itself.” The useful return was not merely a finished deck. A built variation exposed a pacing problem, an inaccurate claim, or a weak transition; that evidence changed the next question while there was still time to act on it.

This is the personal testimony to emphasize: live building and rapid testing let you test hypotheses about which direction might work for the audience. Just as importantly, the exchange helped you ask better questions about what you were building. The loop is therefore more than direction followed by execution; each return can alter the direction itself.

**Build 4 — quotation.** The workbench disappears completely so the sentence owns the frame. Read the complete line slowly: “It seems likely that the contributions of human operators and equipment will blend together so completely in many operations that it will be difficult to separate them neatly in analysis.” In §4, Licklider immediately gives an example in which both person and computer retrieve relevant precedents and the computer suggests a course agreeing with human intuitive judgment. He then says other operations will remain more separable. Preserve both sides: Scene 16's division is analytically useful, while this scene offers situated testimony about how collaboration around a shared artifact can become entangled.

**Transition.** “That entangled period—not machine replacement—is the interim Licklider was excited about.”

**Source.** Exact quotation from the opening of Licklider §4. The storyboard, variants, critique passes, tests, and revision choices describe this project's actual process; they are first-person contemporary testimony, not universal proof that every computer-assisted project is symbiotic.

## Scene 29 — The interim

**Purpose.** Put Licklider's optimism in its actual context and let it carry the spoken ending.

Explain the left and right endpoints carefully. He distinguishes earlier mechanical extension from a distant possibility in which machines might outperform human brains in many functions. He does not make the distant endpoint the subject of this paper. His subject is the interval of people and computers working together.

**Build 1.** Let “10 years?” appear before the band stretches to “500?” The uncertainty is the point. This is not a forecast to score as correct or incorrect.

**Before Build 2.** “Working on this presentation made that possibility feel real to me. I could test a direction while I was still thinking, see what came back, and ask a better question of what we were building. If that is the interim, it is one I feel hopeful about.”

This is where “golden age of human creativity and thought” belongs if you use it. Mark it clearly as your interpretation, not Licklider's literal wording.

**Build 2.** The pulse, mechanisms, and build cue fall away. Read the exact sentence: “The 15 may be 10 or 500, but those years should be intellectually the most creative and exciting in the history of mankind.” Let Licklider's sentence be the last spoken line.

Hold the silence, then advance. Scene 30 is a visual coda, not a second conclusion.

**Source.** Licklider, §1.2.

## Scene 30 — Productive and thriving

**Purpose.** Return gently to the opening relationship in the same photographic, found-film language—without a full-screen image transition, a cartoon pollination sequence, or a second spoken conclusion.

**Base frame.** Hold the real fig tree inside the fixed film gate. The black surrounding field and visible sprockets should make the image feel handled and projected, not expanded into a digital wallpaper.

**Build 1.** A short transport sound advances the strip to the macro photograph of the inside of a fig. The strip moves behind a stationary gate; the photograph itself does not zoom. Let the internal structure sit long enough to register.

**Build 2.** Advance to the real mutualist fig wasp in its habitat. Once the transport settles, reveal “A productive and thriving partnership” beneath the gate. The phrase closely returns to Licklider's description in §1.1.

**Sound and silence.** Remain silent throughout. The transport gives each change a physical cause; beneath it, the opening organic motif, the relationship motif, and a trace of the product motif resolve together. Let the final frame breathe before advancing, then allow the harmony and projector texture to fade through the credits.

**Source and image discipline.** “Productive and thriving partnership” closely quotes §1.1. The three attributed photographs return material already established in the film: the ripe tree, the inside of a cut fig, and a *Pleistodontes* mutualist wasp in a fig. They are an evocative montage, not sequential footage of one act of pollination and not a claim that all three frames depict the same specimen or fig.

## Scene 31 — End credits

**Purpose.** Preserve the found-film fiction while discussion begins.

Advance through the specimens, Licklider and the machines, the audience, and the archive return card. Do not restart the argument and do not replace the sequence with “Questions?”

Let the final harmonic tail and projector mechanism fall away. Leave “PLEASE RETURN THIS FILM TO THE ARCHIVE” visible while people respond.
